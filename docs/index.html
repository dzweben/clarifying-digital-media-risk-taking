DEFT Study 2: from scored data to the published tables

Every Study 2 result in the paper, in the order it was produced, with the reasoning behind each modelling choice and what has to change to run the same pipeline on cohort 1.

Zweben, D., Zaneski, L., Chiu, M., Green, H., Brooks-Justice, J., Dachs, A., Vince, M. A., Olino, T., Marshall, P., Steinberg, L., & Chein, J. M. (2026). Clarifying the association between digital media engagement and risk taking. Technology in Society, 88, 103491. doi:10.1016/j.techsoc.2026.103491

Study 1 and Study 2 use different measures and different samples. This covers Study 2 only — the adolescent and young adult cohorts, PNRT and YRBS risk scores, and the apple-picking foraging task.

What you need

R 4.3 or later, and these packages:

install.packages(c("dplyr", "readr", "tidyr", "psych", "Hmisc",
                   "cocor", "mediation", "lm.beta", "ShapleyValue"))

Put the scripts in a folder next to the data, so that data/, outputs/ and scripts-analyses/ are siblings. Every script resolves its own location and builds paths from there, so it does not matter what your working directory is or whether you run with Rscript, source(), or the RStudio Run button.

study-2/
  data/
    study2_analysis_data.csv
    raw/
      yrbs.csv
      bar.csv
      pnrt.csv
  outputs/                 # created automatically
  scripts-analyses/
    00_setup.R  01_data_prep.R  ...  08_steiger_table6.R

Run 01 through 08 in order. Each one re-runs data prep by itself if outputs/study2_clean.rds is not there yet, so they also work standalone and in any order after the first. Budget a few minutes; script 06 runs six mediations at 5000 bootstrap resamples each and takes nearly all of the time.

PNRT scoring is not the standard scoring

Every PNRT activity is asked twice: have you ever done this, and how often in the past six months. The frequency items are the usual way the instrument is scored. We used the ever items. Each participant's score is the proportion of activities in that set they have ever engaged in — variety, not rate. That is deliberate: the paper's claim is about the breadth of risk behaviour, and scoring it this way keeps the PNRT subtypes on the same footing as the YRBS composite, which is also a proportion of behaviours ever engaged in.

The item-to-valence mapping is not recorded anywhere else, so it is written out here. Note that pnrt_personal is scored negative and pnrt_secret is scored positive, which is not what the variable names suggest.

One discrepancy worth knowing: the published methods section describes eight low-stakes negative activities. The data use seven, and the denominators in the scored columns confirm seven.

Variables built in R

Five variables are constructed in 01_data_prep.R. These are the ones where a scoring decision was made, and they are the ones to check first if cohort 1 numbers look wrong.

VariableHow it is built
mtes_zscore The outcome for the whole paper. Three MTES subscales, each z-scored, then averaged. Z-scoring first is necessary because TimeOnApp is a sum and the other two are item means — averaging them raw would let TimeOnApp dominate.
risk_high_neg Seven YRBS substance behaviours collapsed to ever/never, then averaged. Response coding differs across items, which is why the recodes are not uniform.
exploration Foraging, sign-flipped. Higher apples-per-tree means staying on a patch, which is exploiting; the flip makes the variable read in the direction of its name. Zeros become NA — a participant who harvested nothing did not do the task.
risk_variety_raw Mean of the three subtype proportions, unstandardized. Carried through because the mediation script standardizes within its own subsample.
risk_variety The above, z-scored across the full Study 2 sample.

Outliers are screened at |z| > 3, per variable, and the offending cell is set to NA rather than the row being dropped. A participant with an implausible foraging value still has usable impulsivity and risk data. In the published sample this removes 14 of 2556 cells, about half a percent.

Because mtes_zscore and risk_variety are standardized within whoever is in the file, they are not on the same absolute scale as the published versions. Compare patterns of association across cohorts, not means.

01 · Data preparation builds everything

The only script that constructs variables. Reads the scored dataset, adds the five derived variables described above, renames instrument names to construct names, applies the outlier screen, and writes study2_clean.rds.

The rename step is cosmetic but load-bearing for readability: every downstream script refers to impulsivity and reward_sensitivity rather than barrets.sum and zuckerman.mean.

df <- df_raw %>%
  rename(
    pid                = PID,
    gender             = sex,
    impulsivity        = barrets.sum,
    reward_sensitivity = zuckerman.mean,
    exploration        = foraging,
    social_use         = SocialUsage,
    parasocial_use     = ParasocialUsage,
    risk_low_pos       = positiverisk_ever_mean,
    risk_low_neg       = negativerisk_ever_mean,
    risk_high_neg      = yrbs_var
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    risk_variety_raw = rowMeans(dplyr::select(., risk_low_neg, risk_low_pos, risk_high_neg), na.rm = TRUE),
    risk_variety     = zscore(risk_variety_raw)
  )

The na.rm = TRUE on risk_variety_raw is the thing to watch on cohort 1. If risk_high_neg is missing, this does not error — it quietly averages the two subtypes that remain and produces a composite that is not the published one.

02 · Descriptives Table 4

This is the one script that does not read the cleaned data. Table 4 reports measures on their original scales, so it re-derives from the raw files and skips the outlier screen.

Two things get rescored here. Impulsivity is reported as a mean on the 1–4 scale (2.88 and 2.87) rather than the sum the models use, so the BIS is rebuilt from items with four reverse-worded items flipped. And total risk variety is weighted by item count rather than being a plain mean of the three subtypes:

total_risk_pct = (negativerisk_ever_mean * 7 +
                  positiverisk_ever_mean * 14 +
                  yrbs_var * 7) / (7 + 14 + 7) * 100

That weighting is why the "Risk Taking, Total Variety" row in Table 4 is not the average of the three rows underneath it. Foraging appears here on its raw apples-per-tree scale, before the sign flip.

03 · Correlations Table 5

One published table with two panels, written as two files — stack them to rebuild it. Columns are the outcome and the three cognitive predictors; rows add total risk variety and its three subtypes.

Correlations are pairwise complete, not listwise. Each cell uses everyone who has both variables, so N differs slightly from cell to cell, which is why the table carries significance stars rather than a single N.

Study 2 uses three star levels. Study 1 additionally marked p < .10; Study 2 does not, and format_r() in 00_setup.R reflects that. Changing it will silently desynchronize Table 5 from the paper.

04 · Risk subtype intercorrelations in text

Not a table. These three correlations are quoted at the top of section 3.2.5, and they carry the argument that the subtypes are related but not interchangeable: high- and low-stakes negative risk track each other closely (r = .55 in young adults, .42 in adolescents), while low-stakes positive risk does not track high-stakes negative risk at all (.11 and .07).

That dissociation is what justifies analysing the subtypes separately for the rest of the paper instead of collapsing them.

05 · Shapley value regression in text

The predictors are correlated, so ordinary betas depend on entry order and each predictor's apparent importance shifts with whatever else is in the model. Shapley averages each predictor's contribution to R² across every possible ordering. The result sums to the model R² and does not depend on how you ordered the steps.

Read the output as share of explained variance, not as effect size. The adolescent model explains 20.8% of variance in MTES; risk variety accounts for roughly half of that 20.8%, not half of MTES.

This uses risk_variety, the composite, rather than the three subtypes. Feeding in three heavily overlapping risk terms would split one construct's contribution across them and make every risk share look small. The na.omit() here is necessarily listwise: Shapley refits over every subset of predictors and needs one constant sample to compare them against.

06 · Indirect effects Table S3

Cognitive trait → risk variety → SSMU, run separately for each trait and each cohort. Six mediations, 5000 bootstrap resamples each.

Direction is worth stating plainly: risk taking is the mediator, social media engagement is the outcome. The data are cross-sectional, so this tests statistical accounting, not causal sequence. The paper words these as "accounted for" throughout and never as "led to" — keep that.

Predictors are standardized after subsetting to cohort and after na.omit(), so every variable is z-scored within the exact sample the models are fit on:

med_df <- data %>%
  dplyr::select(mtes_zscore, risk_variety_raw, reward_sensitivity, impulsivity, exploration) %>%
  mutate(
    risk_variety_z       = zscore(risk_variety_raw),
    reward_sensitivity_z = zscore(reward_sensitivity),
    impulsivity_z        = zscore(impulsivity),
    exploration_z        = zscore(exploration)
  ) %>%
  na.omit()

Standardizing earlier — in data prep, across both cohorts — would put adolescents and young adults on a shared scale and change the coefficients. This is the reason risk_variety_raw exists.

set.seed(2025) is at the top. Without it the confidence intervals move on every run and the published values will not reproduce.

The proportion mediated exceeds 100% for reward sensitivity in both cohorts (112.4% and 110.6%). That happens when the direct and indirect paths have opposite signs. It is not an error, the paper reports it alongside a nonsignificant p for the proportion, and it should not be "fixed".

07 · Hierarchical regression Table S4

The question: once you account for who someone is dispositionally, does what they do still explain anything about their social media engagement?

  1. sex + impulsivity
  2. + reward sensitivity
  3. + exploration
  4. + the three risk subtypes

Sex is in from Step 1 as a covariate, not a predictor of interest. Entering the cognitive traits before risk is the conservative ordering — it gives the dispositional account first claim on shared variance, so whatever risk picks up in Step 4 is over and above traits. The ΔR² at Step 4 is the test.

Listwise deletion happens once, up front, so all four steps are fit on the same participants. Without that, the step-to-step comparisons would be across different samples and anova() would be meaningless:

d <- data %>% dplyr::select(dplyr::all_of(model_vars)) %>% na.omit()

Betas in the table are standardized via lm.beta; the SE and t columns come from the unstandardized fit.

The two cohorts diverge here, and it is the substantive finding: for young adults, risk does not add over traits, because impulsivity absorbs it. For adolescents, it does.

08 · Social versus parasocial Table 6

If the link between social media and risk were just screen time, it would not matter what kind of platform the time was spent on. It does. Risk tracks reciprocal platforms and not one-sided ones, in both cohorts.

Steiger's z is the right test because the two correlations being compared share a variable — the risk subtype — and come from the same people. Comparing two r values directly would ignore that dependency and overstate the difference. The test needs a third correlation, social use with parasocial use, to know how dependent they are:

test <- cocor.dep.groups.overlap(
  r.jk = r_social,             # risk x social
  r.jh = r_parasocial,         # risk x parasocial
  r.kh = r_social_parasocial,  # social x parasocial
  n    = n
)

The sign of z is flipped in the output so that a negative value means social > parasocial, matching the direction printed in Table 6. cocor orders the comparison the other way.

Running this on cohort 1

High-stakes risk will not be there

Cohort 1 has no YRBS substance scoring, so risk_high_neg does not exist. That variable is load-bearing in four places: a row in Table 5, a row in Table 6, a term in Step 4 of Table S4, and one of three components of risk_variety.

The failure mode is silent, not loud. risk_variety_raw uses na.rm = TRUE, so a missing high-stakes score does not error — it produces a two-component composite that looks fine and is not comparable to the published one. Decide explicitly and state it: either redefine total risk variety as a two-subtype composite and report it as such, or substitute whatever high-stakes measure cohort 1 does carry. Study 1 used the BRP rather than the YRBS for exactly this variable, so a BRP-based version is precedented within the paper.

What to edit, concretely: drop risk_high_neg from row_vars in 03, from predictors in 08, from model_vars and m4 in 07, and remove both the term and its weight from the total_risk_pct denominator in 02. Leaving the 7 in that denominator understates the percentage.

A two-predictor Step 4 is not comparable to the published ΔR². Report it as its own result, not as a replication.

Participants without a phone

The MTES composite leans on TimeOnApp.sum, which is a sum across platforms. Someone with no phone who selects nothing sums to zero, and after z-scoring lands at the bottom of the distribution — which encodes "no phone" as "lightest user."

Whether that is a true floor or should be NA is a decision, not a bug. Check it with Jason before running anything; Lina and Jason have almost certainly set a rule for cohort 1 already. Whatever the rule is, it needs to be applied before mtes_zscore is computed, because the z-scoring is relative to whoever is in the file.

Exclusions

Anyone missing one of the at-home cognition measures — Zuckerman, BIS, foraging — is out. In this pipeline that happens through listwise deletion inside each model rather than as an explicit step, which means N varies a little between tables. If cohort 1 has more missingness than the published sample, it is worth adding an explicit filter and reporting one N, rather than letting it vary silently.

Cohort split

Age group was encoded in the PID: 2xxx adolescents, 3xxx and above young adults. If that convention does not carry over, change it in one place at the bottom of 00_setup.R — every script splits through these two functions rather than hardcoding the comparison:

is_young_adult <- function(d) d$pid > 3000
is_adolescent  <- function(d) d$pid < 3000

Defensible alternatives

Two choices in the pipeline could reasonably have gone another way. Either alternative is fine if it is stated.

Structuring Step 4

All three risk subtypes enter Step 4 together. They correlate between .25 and .55, so each beta is that subtype's unique contribution net of the others, and none reaches significance in the young adult model even though the block as a whole sits near threshold.

The alternatives: put the composite in Step 4 and the subtypes in Step 5, which tests "does risk matter" before "which kind of risk" and avoids attributing shared risk variance to any one subtype; or run three separate Step 4 models, one subtype each, which gives the cleanest read on each subtype but means the betas are no longer unique contributions and you are running three tests where the paper ran one.

What is not defensible is trying each ordering and reporting whichever one makes risk significant.

Scoring the PNRT by frequency

Using the frequency items instead of the ever items is the standard scoring and would be a legitimate robustness check — the columns are in pnrt.csv already. It answers a different question, though: rate rather than breadth. If cohort 1 is scored on frequency, it is not a replication of this paper's risk variables, and the YRBS composite would need the same treatment to stay consistent.
