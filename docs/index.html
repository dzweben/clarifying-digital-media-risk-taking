The Study 2 analyses from Zweben et al. (2026), what each one is for, and the order they were run in, so you can do the same on cohort 1.

Zweben, D., Zaneski, L., Chiu, M., Green, H., Brooks-Justice, J., Dachs, A., Vince, M. A., Olino, T., Marshall, P., Steinberg, L., & Chein, J. M. (2026). Clarifying the association between digital media engagement and risk taking. Technology in Society, 88, 103491. doi:10.1016/j.techsoc.2026.103491

Study 2 only. Two cohorts out of LITE: adolescents 11 to 17, young adults 18 to 21. Study 1 is a different sample with different instruments.

The outcome is SSMU, social media engagement, built from three MTES subscales. The predictors are impulsivity (BIS), reward sensitivity (Zuckerman sensation seeking), exploration (the apple-picking foraging task), and risk taking split three ways: low-stakes positive and low-stakes negative from the PNRT, and high-stakes negative from the YRBS substance items. Platform time is split into social and parasocial.

Score everything and screen outliers however the lab does it. This document picks up from a scored file, one row per participant. For reference, DEFT screened at |z| > 3 per variable and blanked the individual cell rather than dropping the row, so one bad foraging value did not cost that participant everything else.

Two things about how we scored are not standard, and are not written down anywhere else. They come first, because both of them affect everything after.

Caveat 1: the PNRT

Positive risk, negative risk, and total variety all come off the PNRT, and we score it differently than it is normally scored.

Every PNRT activity is asked twice: have you ever done this, and if so, how often in the past six months. Variety normally comes off the second question. An activity counts as 1 if there was any engagement in the past six months and 0 if there was none.

We could not do that. For the first six months of LITE, before Avi and I were on it, the frequency follow-up gave participants no way to answer zero times once they had already said yes to ever having done the activity. For those participants the frequency item cannot separate recent engagement from none.

So we score variety off the ever items, and we have kept doing that since. The zero option may well have been fixed by now. Do not switch. Cohort 1 has to be scored the same way for the two to be comparable.

Each score is the proportion of activities in its set that the participant has ever done. The item lists:

Six of them, run in this order.

Two things about the order:

Shapley runs before the regression and does not replace it. The predictors are correlated, so betas depend on entry order and Shapley does not. The regression then asks whether risk adds anything once the traits have had first claim on the shared variance.

The subtype intercorrelations run before the subtype analyses. They show the three kinds of risk are related but not interchangeable, which is why the last step splits risk into three predictors instead of using the composite.

Cohort 1: no high-stakes risk

DEFT had low-stakes positive, low-stakes negative, and high-stakes negative. Cohort 1 has no YRBS substance scoring, so it has the first two.

Delete the risk_high_neg line from the column map and the rest follows. Total variety becomes the mean of two pieces, the high-stakes row drops out of Tables 5 and 6, and the last regression step tests two risk predictors. That ΔR² is a new number rather than a replication of the published one, so report it that way.

Do not leave the column mapped and empty. Total variety uses na.rm = TRUE, so it will average whatever is there and give you a number that looks fine.

Step 0: Variables the analysis builds

Everything else arrives scored. These three are analysis decisions, so they happen here.

SSMU composite. The outcome for the whole paper. The three MTES subscales sit on different scales, since time is a sum across platforms and the other two are item means, so each gets z-scored before averaging. Otherwise time dominates.

It is z-scored within whoever is in the file, so it is not on the same absolute scale across datasets. Compare patterns of association, not means. It also has to be built before anything is joined on, since a join that changes the row count moves the mean and SD with it.

Exploration. Apples per tree. Staying on a patch means exploiting, so higher raw values mean less exploration. Sign-flipped so the variable reads in the direction of its name. Zeros become NA, since someone who harvested nothing did not do the task.

Total risk variety. The mean of the risk subtypes, then z-scored. The unstandardized version is kept because step 4 re-standardizes within its own subsample.

Step 2: Correlations (Table 5)

One published table, two panels. Pairwise complete rather than listwise, so each cell uses everyone who has both variables. That is why the table carries stars instead of a single N.

main_vars <- c("mtes_zscore", "impulsivity", "reward_sensitivity", "exploration")
row_vars  <- c(main_vars, "risk_variety", risk_subtypes)

get_r_p <- function(d, x, y) {
  d2 <- d[, c(x, y)]; d2 <- d2[complete.cases(d2), ]
  if (nrow(d2) < 3) return(list(r = NA_real_, p = NA_real_))
  t <- cor.test(d2[[x]], d2[[y]], method = "pearson")
  list(r = unname(t$estimate), p = t$p.value)
}

build_corr_table <- function(d) {
  r_mat <- matrix(NA_real_, length(row_vars), length(main_vars),
                  dimnames = list(row_vars, main_vars))
  p_mat <- r_mat
  for (rv in row_vars) for (cv in main_vars) {
    if (rv == cv) next                       # diagonal stays blank, as in the paper
    res <- get_r_p(d, rv, cv); r_mat[rv, cv] <- res$r; p_mat[rv, cv] <- res$p
  }
  out <- matrix("", nrow(r_mat), ncol(r_mat), dimnames = dimnames(r_mat))
  for (i in seq_len(nrow(r_mat))) for (j in seq_len(ncol(r_mat)))
    if (!is.na(r_mat[i, j])) out[i, j] <- format_r(r_mat[i, j], p_mat[i, j])
  as.data.frame(cbind(Variable = rownames(out), out))
}

Step 3: Shapley

The predictors are correlated, so ordinary betas shift depending on what else is in the model and in what order. Shapley averages each predictor's contribution to R² over every possible ordering, so it sums to the model R² and does not depend on entry order.

Read it as share of explained variance, not effect size. The adolescent model explains 20.8% of the variance in MTES, and risk variety accounts for about half of that 20.8%, not half of MTES.

It uses total variety rather than the three subtypes. Three overlapping risk terms would split one construct's contribution across them and make every risk share look small. The na.omit has to be listwise, since Shapley refits over every subset of predictors and needs one constant sample.

First the subtype intercorrelations. They are reported in the text, and they are why the subtypes get treated separately later.

run_corr <- function(d, x, y, cohort) {
  d2 <- d[, c(x, y)]; d2 <- d2[complete.cases(d2), ]
  if (nrow(d2) < 3) return(data.frame(cohort, var1 = x, var2 = y, r = NA, p = NA, n = nrow(d2)))
  t <- cor.test(d2[[x]], d2[[y]], method = "pearson")
  data.frame(cohort, var1 = x, var2 = y, r = round(unname(t$estimate), 3),
             p = round(t$p.value, 4), n = nrow(d2))
}

run_shapley <- function(data, label) {
  sd_ <- data %>%
    dplyr::select(mtes_zscore, risk_variety, reward_sensitivity,
                  impulsivity, exploration, gender) %>%
    na.omit()
  sv <- shapleyvalue(y = sd_$mtes_zscore,
                     x = sd_ %>% dplyr::select(-mtes_zscore) %>% as.data.frame())
  out <- as.data.frame(sv)
  # Row 1 = raw Shapley value; row 2 = share of model R2, as a percent.
  out <- rbind(Shapley_Value = out[1, ], Percent_R2 = round(as.numeric(out[2, ]) * 100, 2))
  write_csv(as.data.frame(out), file.path(output_dir, paste0("study2_shapley_", label, ".csv")))
  out
}

Step 4: Indirect effects (Table S3)

Trait to risk variety to SSMU, separately for each trait and each cohort. Six mediations at 5000 bootstrap resamples each. This one takes a few minutes.

Risk taking is the mediator and SSMU is the outcome. The data are cross-sectional, so this is statistical accounting rather than causal order. The paper says "accounted for" throughout and never "led to". Keep that.

Predictors get standardized after subsetting to cohort and after na.omit, so everything is z-scored within the exact sample the models are fit on. Standardizing earlier, across both cohorts, would put adolescents and young adults on a shared scale and change the coefficients.

set.seed(2025)   # bootstrap CIs move on every run without this

run_mediation_set <- function(data, label) {
  m <- data %>%
    dplyr::select(mtes_zscore, risk_variety_raw, reward_sensitivity, impulsivity, exploration) %>%
    mutate(risk_variety_z = zscore(risk_variety_raw), reward_sensitivity_z = zscore(reward_sensitivity),
           impulsivity_z = zscore(impulsivity), exploration_z = zscore(exploration)) %>%
    na.omit()

  # Written out longhand rather than looped. mediate() re-evaluates the model
  # calls internally, and building the formulas programmatically breaks it.
  mi <- mediate(lm(risk_variety_z ~ impulsivity_z, data = m),
                lm(mtes_zscore ~ impulsivity_z + risk_variety_z, data = m),
                treat = "impulsivity_z", mediator = "risk_variety_z", boot = TRUE, sims = 5000)
  mr <- mediate(lm(risk_variety_z ~ reward_sensitivity_z, data = m),
                lm(mtes_zscore ~ reward_sensitivity_z + risk_variety_z, data = m),
                treat = "reward_sensitivity_z", mediator = "risk_variety_z", boot = TRUE, sims = 5000)
  me <- mediate(lm(risk_variety_z ~ exploration_z, data = m),
                lm(mtes_zscore ~ exploration_z + risk_variety_z, data = m),
                treat = "exploration_z", mediator = "risk_variety_z", boot = TRUE, sims = 5000)

  # d0 = indirect (ACME), z0 = direct (ADE), n0 = proportion mediated.
  ex <- function(x, nm) data.frame(
    Predictor = nm,
    Indirect  = sprintf("%.2f (%.2f, %.2f), %.3f", x$d0, x$d0.ci[1], x$d0.ci[2], x$d0.p),
    Direct    = sprintf("%.2f (%.2f, %.2f), %.2f", x$z0, x$z0.ci[1], x$z0.ci[2], x$z0.p),
    Prop      = sprintf("%.1f%%, %.2f", x$n0 * 100, x$n0.p), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

  out <- bind_rows(ex(mi, "impulsivity"), ex(mr, "reward_sensitivity"), ex(me, "exploration"))
  write_csv(out, file.path(output_dir, paste0("study2_mediation_", label, ".csv")))
  out
}

Step 5: Hierarchical regression (Table S4)

Once you account for who someone is dispositionally, does what they do still explain anything about their media engagement?

  1. sex + impulsivity
  2. + reward sensitivity
  3. + exploration
  4. + the risk subtypes

Sex is a covariate from the start, not a predictor of interest. Traits go in before risk on purpose. That gives the dispositional account first claim on the shared variance, so anything risk picks up in the last step is over and above them. That ΔR² is the test.

Listwise deletion happens once, up front, so all four steps are fit on the same people. Otherwise the ΔR² comparisons run across different samples and anova() means nothing.

build_hier_table <- function(data, out_name) {
  model_vars <- c("mtes_zscore", "gender", "impulsivity", "reward_sensitivity",
                  "exploration", risk_entry_order)
  d <- data %>% dplyr::select(dplyr::all_of(model_vars)) %>% na.omit()

  base <- "mtes_zscore ~ gender + impulsivity"
  f <- c(base, paste(base, "+ reward_sensitivity"),
         paste(base, "+ reward_sensitivity + exploration"),
         paste(base, "+ reward_sensitivity + exploration +",
               paste(risk_entry_order, collapse = " + ")))

  models     <- lapply(f, function(x) lm(as.formula(x), data = d))
  models_std <- lapply(models, lm.beta)          # table betas are standardized
  anova_res  <- do.call(anova, models)

  fb <- function(b, p) {
    s <- if (p < .001) "***" else if (p < .01) "**" else if (p < .05) "*" else if (p < .10) "+" else ""
    sprintf("%.2f%s", b, s)
  }

  rows <- list()
  for (i in seq_along(models)) {
    su <- summary(models[[i]]); ss <- summary(models_std[[i]])
    rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- data.frame(Step = paste0("Step ", i), Predictor = "",
                                           Beta = "", SE = "", t = "", p = "", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
    for (j in 2:nrow(su$coefficients)) {         # start at 2 to skip the intercept
      rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- data.frame(
        Step = "", Predictor = rownames(su$coefficients)[j],
        Beta = fb(ss$coefficients[j, "Standardized"], su$coefficients[j, "Pr(>|t|)"]),
        # SE and t come from the unstandardized fit; only beta is standardized.
        SE = sprintf("%.2f", su$coefficients[j, "Std. Error"]),
        t  = sprintf("%.2f", su$coefficients[j, "t value"]),
        p  = format_p_num(su$coefficients[j, "Pr(>|t|)"]), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
    }
    r2 <- su$r.squared; fs <- su$fstatistic
    pv <- pf(fs[1], fs[2], fs[3], lower.tail = FALSE)
    dr <- if (i > 1) paste0(", Delta R2 = ", sprintf("%.3f", r2 - summary(models[[i-1]])$r.squared),
                            ", p = ", format_p_num(anova_res$`Pr(>F)`[i])) else ""
    rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- data.frame(
      Step = "", Predictor = paste0("R2 = ", sprintf("%.3f", r2), ", F(", fs[2], ", ", fs[3], ") = ",
                                    sprintf("%.2f", fs[1]), ", p = ", format_p_num(pv), dr),
      Beta = "", SE = "", t = "", p = "", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
  }
  out <- bind_rows(rows); write_csv(out, file.path(output_dir, out_name)); out
}

The cohorts differ here, and that is the main finding. For young adults risk does not add over traits, because impulsivity absorbs it. For adolescents it does.

All the subtypes go into the last step together. They correlate between .25 and .55, so each beta is that subtype's unique contribution net of the others, and none of them reaches significance in the young adult model even though the block as a whole sits near threshold. Two other defensible ways to do it: total variety in step 4 with the subtypes in a step 5, which asks whether risk matters before asking which kind; or three separate step 4 models, one subtype each, which reads each subtype cleanly but runs three tests where the paper ran one. Either is fine as long as you say which you did. Do not run all of them and report whichever one makes risk significant.

Cohort 1: two risk predictors in the last step instead of three, so that ΔR² is a new result rather than a replication.

Step 6: Social versus parasocial (Table 6)

If the effect were just screen time, platform type would not matter. It does. Risk tracks reciprocal platforms and not one-sided ones, in both cohorts.

Steiger's z is the right test because the two correlations being compared share a variable, the risk subtype, and come from the same people. Comparing two r values directly ignores that and overstates the difference. The test needs a third correlation, social with parasocial, to know how dependent they are.

run_steiger <- function(data) {
  rows <- list()
  for (pred in risk_subtypes) {
    r_s  <- cor(data[[pred]], data$social_use,     use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
    r_p  <- cor(data[[pred]], data$parasocial_use, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
    r_sp <- cor(data$social_use, data$parasocial_use, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
    n <- nrow(na.omit(data[, c(pred, "social_use", "parasocial_use")]))
    st <- cocor.dep.groups.overlap(r.jk = r_s, r.jh = r_p, r.kh = r_sp, n = n)@steiger1980
    rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- data.frame(
      Risk_Subtype = pred, r_social = round(r_s, 2), r_parasocial = round(r_p, 2),
      # Sign flipped so a negative z means social > parasocial, matching Table 6.
      # cocor orders the comparison the other way.
      steiger_z = round(-1 * st$statistic, 2), p_value = round(st$p.value, 3), n = n)
  }
  bind_rows(rows)
}
